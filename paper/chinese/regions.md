## The Four Written Standards

| Variant | Script | Quotes | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mainland (中国大陆) | 简体 | “ ” ‘ ’ | 普通话 vocabulary; 拼音 romanisation; the default |
| Taiwan (台灣) | 繁體 | 「」『』 | 國語 vocabulary; distinct technical terms; softer, more particled written tone |
| Hong Kong (香港) | 繁體 | 「」『』 | Standard Written Chinese for formal text, but HK-specific vocabulary and English loans; written Cantonese in informal text |
| Singapore / Malaysia (新马) | 简体 | “ ” | Mainland script, but its own vocabulary and heavy English and Malay borrowing |

The script tells you almost nothing on its own. A 繁體 text written with mainland vocabulary reads as a machine conversion, which is the most common failure in this whole area.

## Conversion Is Not Substitution

Converting 简体 → 繁體 has three layers, and glyph converters do only the first:

1. **Characters.** 学 → 學, 国 → 國. Automatable, except for the one-to-many set below.
2. **Vocabulary.** 视频 → 影片, 软件 → 軟體, 网络 → 網路. Not automatable in general, because the mapping is context-dependent: 信息 is 資訊 when it means information and 訊息 when it means a message.
3. **Manner.** Sentence length, particle density, degree of directness. Taiwanese written Chinese is gentler and more particled than mainland business writing; Hong Kong formal writing is more clipped and carries more English.

A reader spots layer 2 in one line and layer 3 in one paragraph.

## One-to-Many Characters

Where one simplified character maps to several traditional ones. A converter guesses, and guesses wrong on exactly these:

| 云 | 雲 / 云 | 雲端 cloud · 人云亦云 to say |

## Tone and Manner

- **Taiwanese written Chinese** uses more sentence-final particles even in business writing (囉, 喔, 呢), softer requests (麻煩您, 再麻煩您了), and 您 less often than the mainland while being no less polite. 好的 is often 好的喔 or 好唷 in service register.
- **Mainland business writing** is more compressed and more direct, especially in tech, and uses more untranslated English acronyms.
- **Hong Kong formal writing** carries more English inline and more legal-register Chinese, a legacy of bilingual administration; informal writing switches to Cantonese entirely.
- Taiwanese internet slang is its own set (母湯, 傻眼貓咪, 484, 484 = 是不是) and mainland slang does not carry across — using 绝绝子 in Taiwanese copy is as visible as using 母湯 on 微博 (`slang.md`).

## Written Cantonese

Standard Written Chinese (書面語) and written Cantonese (粵文) are different systems, and Hong Kong readers use both depending on context:

| Function | 書面語 | 粵文 |
|---|---|---|
| to be | 是 | 係 |
| not | 不 | 唔 |
| possessive / attributive | 的 | 嘅 |
| perfective | 了 | 咗 |
| this / that | 这 / 那 | 呢 / 嗰 |
| they | 他们 | 佢哋 |
| to eat | 吃 | 食 |

- Newspapers, official notices, business letters and school writing use 書面語 with traditional characters. Chat, social media, advertising and dialogue use 粵文.
- Mixing them mid-text is a deliberate register move in Hong Kong writing, not an error — an ad that opens in 書面語 and lands the punchline in 粵文 is doing something on purpose.
- Writing 粵文 requires the Cantonese-specific characters, not Mandarin words with Cantonese pronunciation. If those characters are unavailable, write 書面語 rather than a hybrid.

## Singapore and Malaysia

- Simplified script, mainland-derived, with local vocabulary: 巴刹 (market), 组屋 (public housing), 德士 (taxi), 乐龄 (senior citizens), 固本 (coupon), 罗厘 (lorry).
- Heavy code-switching with English and Malay in informal writing; formal writing is closer to mainland standard than Taiwanese writing is.
- 华语 rather than 普通话 or 國語 as the name of the language.
- Date and money formats follow local convention (SGD, RM), not mainland.

## Mixed-Audience Writing

When one text must serve readers across variants:

- **Pick a primary market and write for it.** A text that avoids every divergent word to be neutral reads as translated into nothing. This is the same trade-off as neutral Spanish.
- If both a mainland and a Taiwan version are genuinely needed, produce two texts. Converting one produces layer-1 conversion only, and the reader in the secondary market pays for it.
- Where a single version is unavoidable, prefer the shared word where one exists (电脑 works everywhere; 计算机 and 電腦 do not both), keep numbers and dates in digit form, and avoid slang from either market.
- Glossaries split by variant the moment a second variant appears (`memory-template.md`) — one term table serving two variants cannot be read while writing in either.

## Regional Speech Inside the Mainland

Relevant when writing dialogue, marketing for a specific city, or matching a colleague's voice:

- **儿化** (这儿, 一会儿, 玩儿, 门儿清) is northern; southern readers hear it as Beijing colour. Neutral national writing drops it (SKILL.md, Where Experts Disagree).
- **贼** as an intensifier (贼好吃) is northeastern; **蛮** (蛮好的) is Jiangnan and Wuhan; **老** as an intensifier (老好了) is northeastern too.
- 咱们 (inclusive we, includes the listener) versus 我们 — 咱们 is northern and warm; southern writers use 我们 for both.
- Sichuanese, Northeastern and Cantonese words leak into national internet language constantly (巴适, 整活, 搞掂) and are read as flavour rather than as dialect.

## What Gets Written Down

- **The variant and script decision, and any per-thread exception** — 简体 mainland by default but 繁體 for one client — → `## Profile` in `memory.md`, and the exception as a note on that person's `## Recipients` row.
- **Every term whose rendering differs by variant** → a `### Terms` row with the variant column filled. The second variant splits the glossary immediately (`memory-template.md`).
- **A one-to-many character that a converter got wrong in the user's own text** → `### Corrections`, because the same converter will do it again.
