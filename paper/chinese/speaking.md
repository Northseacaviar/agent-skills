# Spoken Chinese — Scripts Meant to Be Said Aloud

**Before writing a script for a named occasion or audience**, read the `## Recipients` rows for the people who will hear it and any `artifacts/` script `## Boxes` names for a similar occasion. A 敬酒词 or a self-introduction that worked is reused, not rebuilt.

Text written to be read is not text written to be heard. Chinese widens that gap more than English does, because homophones make written-style vocabulary genuinely ambiguous out loud.

## What Changes When It Is Spoken

| Written | Spoken |
|---|---|
| Long pre-nominal attributives | Broken into clauses; the topic stated first and then commented on |
| 该 / 此 / 其 | 这个 / 那个 / 他的 |
| Single-syllable literary words (故, 若, 亦) | Two-syllable colloquial equivalents (所以, 如果, 也) |
| No repetition | Repetition of the topic — 这个事儿啊，这个事儿其实… |
| Precise connectives | 然后, 就是, 那个 as verbal glue |
| Sentence-final 。 | Sentence-final particles |
| Numbers as digits | Numbers said in full, with 幺 for 1 in digit strings (`numbers-and-names.md`) |

Sentence length: aim for clauses of roughly 10-15 characters between natural pauses. A 40-character sentence that reads fine on paper cannot be said in one breath, and the speaker will break it in the wrong place.

## Homophones Force Word Choice

Written Chinese disambiguates by character; speech cannot. Words that are clear on the page and ambiguous in the air need replacing:

| Ambiguous aloud | Say instead |
|---|---|
| 期中 / 期终 | 期中考试 / 期末考试 |
| 全部 / 全不 | 都 / 一个都没有 |
| 十 / 是 (in noisy conditions) | 拾, or restate the number |
| 4 / 死 in a list of numbers | Say digits as 幺二三四, slowly, or avoid the position |
| 权利 / 权力 | 名词 plus a clarifier: 法律上的权利 |
| 致辞 / 制词 | Rephrase entirely |

The general fix is to add a syllable: two-syllable and three-syllable words are far less ambiguous than one-syllable ones, which is exactly why spoken Chinese prefers them.

## Self-Introduction

Four beats, in this order, adjusted for the setting:

1. **Name, with the characters explained if it will be written down.** Chinese speakers routinely gloss their own name: 我叫王建国，建设的建，国家的国. A non-Chinese name gets its transliteration and, if useful, what it means.
2. **Where you sit** — company, team, role. In a business setting this precedes anything personal.
3. **Why you are in the room** — the connection to the audience or the occasion.
4. **A hook or a closing courtesy** — 请多指教 (formal, humble), 以后多交流 (peer), 有什么需要随时找我 (service).

Length is set by the setting: 30 seconds at a business meeting, 10 seconds around a dinner table, 90 seconds when the introduction is the point. Going long is the standard mistake.

请多关照 is a Japanese-influenced formula that appears in Chinese but reads as slightly foreign; 请多指教 is the native equivalent.

## Phone and Voice Messages

- Answering: 喂，你好 (neutral) · 喂 (casual) · 您好，这里是<公司> (business).
- Identify yourself in the first sentence: 你好，我是<公司>的<姓名>. A caller who does not is assumed to be selling something.
- State the purpose and the length up front: 想跟您确认一个事儿，两分钟.
- Closing: 那就这样 / 好的，就先这样 / 回头再联系 / 拜拜. Hanging up without a closing formula is abrupt in every register.
- Voice messages: announce the length first (`chat.md`), one topic per message, and never for anything the listener will need to search for later.

## Presentations

- Chinese business presentations open with the conclusion or the agenda, not with a story. 今天主要讲三件事 followed by the three is the expected opening.
- 各位领导、各位同事，大家好 is the standard address when senior people are present; the ordering of the groups is itself a courtesy and getting it backwards is noticed.
- Signposting between sections is more explicit than in English: 第一部分讲完了，接下来看第二部分.
- Slides carry short phrases; Chinese characters are dense and a full sentence at slide size is unreadable (`social-media.md` on the same constraint).
- Q&A: 这个问题问得很好 is a real formula, not filler, and buys thinking time. 我回去确认一下再答复您 is the correct answer to something unknown — inventing an answer in front of a Chinese audience costs more face than admitting the gap.

## 敬酒词 and Toasts

- Order matters: the most senior person is toasted first, and the toaster's glass is held lower than theirs when clinking. This is a physical convention that the words assume.
- Shape: address → the reason for the occasion → a good wish → 干杯 or 我先干为敬.
- 我先干为敬 means the speaker drinks first and fully; it is a commitment, not a phrase.
- Standard wishes: 身体健康 · 工作顺利 · 生意兴隆 · 万事如意 · 恭喜发财 (New Year and business) · 前程似锦 (to someone younger starting out) · 百年好合 / 早生贵子 (weddings).
- Four-character parallelism is genuine oratory here, unlike on the page (`ai-tells.md`): 感谢各位拨冗出席，祝大家身体健康、万事如意.
- Declining alcohol: 我以茶代酒 is the standard and is accepted; a medical reason (在吃药) closes the matter.
- Length: 30 seconds at a business dinner. A long toast holds the table's glasses in the air.

## 主持 — Hosting

- Opening: 尊敬的各位领导、各位来宾，大家上午好。欢迎参加…
- Transitions: 接下来有请… / 让我们以热烈的掌声欢迎…
- 有请 is the formula for inviting someone up and is not optional.
- Closing: 今天的活动到此结束，感谢各位的参与。
- The host's register is one rung above the audience's, always, and the host never jokes at the expense of anyone senior present.

## Video Scripts

- The first line is the hook, spoken (`social-media.md`). Everything after it competes with the scroll.
- Write for the ear at speaking speed: roughly 200-250 characters per minute of Mandarin at a natural pace, faster for 抖音-style delivery. Use it to size a script, then read it aloud and time it — the estimate is a starting point, not a spec.
- Repeat the subject at the start of each new point; a listener who missed a clause cannot scroll back.
- On-screen text is a summary of the speech, not a transcript of it.

## Delivery Notes for a Non-Native Speaker

Only relevant when the user will be speaking the text themselves and asks:

- Mark the tone-sensitive pairs in the script where a mistake changes the meaning materially (买/卖, 问/吻, 汤/糖).
- Neutral tone matters more than learners expect: 东西 (thing) versus 东西 with a full fourth tone (east and west) is heard immediately.
- 一 and 不 change tone before certain syllables; this is automatic for a native and worth marking in a script that has to be right.
- The 儿化 in a script is a regional choice (`regions.md`) — do not add it to a script the speaker will not say naturally.
- A pinyin gloss for the whole script is what `pinyin_gloss: all` produces; a gloss only on the hard words is `new-words` and is what most speakers actually want.

## What Gets Written Down

- **A script that was delivered** — a toast, a self-introduction, a presentation opening → `artifacts/script-<occasion>.md` with the date and the occasion, and its `## Boxes` line in the same turn. These recur annually and rebuilding them from scratch every year is pure waste.
- **A pinyin or pronunciation guide prepared for a specific script** → `artifacts/` alongside it, not inside the script.
- **How it landed** — laughter, silence, a correction from someone present → a row in `pieces/<year>.md` with the length in minutes, and anything sharp in `## Pain Points`.
- **The user's own pronunciation trouble spots**, observed across sessions → `## Profile`, so future scripts route around them rather than re-teaching them.
